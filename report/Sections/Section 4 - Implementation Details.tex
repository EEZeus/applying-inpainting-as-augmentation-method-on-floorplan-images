\section{Implementation and Evaluation Details}

The project ran in four phases. Phase 1 audited and de-duplicated the source
COCO data and froze a project-aware split. Phase 2 built the generation stack
and the augmented datasets. Phase 3 ran the controlled Mask R-CNN matrix. After
the first single-seed pass proved uninformative, it was extended to three seeds
and then to a rotated held-out cross-validation. Phase 4 tested whether the
verdict depended on the backbone, first by fine-tuning SAM 3 and then by
splitting the task into a class-agnostic detector plus a frozen DINOv2
classifier. The ordering was not planned in advance. Each phase responded to
what the previous one had failed to show.

\subsection{Insights derived from previous work}

Three readings of the prior literature shaped the design. First, Ghiasi et al.\
\cite{ghiasi2021copypaste} report that a crude pixel transplant with no
generative model beats more elaborate composition schemes. That made a
non-generative control mandatory. Without a copy-paste arm, any gain from
diffusion inpainting could equally be a gain from placing a rare polygon in a
new geometric context, which has nothing to do with the generator. The matrix
was therefore structured so that the diffusion treatment is always flanked by a
copy-paste control, which uses the same pairing and target polygon but no
generated pixels, and by a feather-blend control, which smooths the seam without
diffusion.

Second, Fill-Up \cite{shin2023fillup} and DoGE \cite{wang2024doge} diagnose the
same failure mode, where a frozen text-to-image model asked for an
out-of-distribution class produces images that do not lie in the target domain.
They propose opposite fixes: adapt the generator, or steer the frozen generator
with a domain-gap embedding. The LoRA branch of this project implements the
first of these on Stable Diffusion 1.5 inpainting
\cite{rombach2022ldm,hu2022lora}. A preliminary check confirmed the diagnosis
applied here. Prompting the pretrained model with ``architectural technical
floor plan, Versammlungsraum, 2D line drawing'' produced perspective furniture
and photographic texture inside the masked room instead of 2D symbols, as
Figures~\ref{fig:qastrategya} and \ref{fig:loraval} show.

Third, the literature evaluates these methods in a regime with two to three
orders of magnitude more data. LVIS \cite{gupta2019lvis} calls a category rare
when it appears in fewer than 10 of about 100k training images. Our rare
categories appear 3--8 times in a 35-image training split and are tested on 1--6
instances each. This motivated the class-agnostic track and, later, the
cross-validation and the two-stage decomposition. A null result would only be
useful if it came with evidence about why it was null, and about whether the
measurement could have detected an effect at all.

\subsection{Dataset Description and preprocessing}
\label{sec:dataset}

The source is a COCO-format collection of German fire-brigade floor plans with
60 images, 1709 polygon annotations and 19 room categories. A Phase-1 audit
verified that every referenced image file exists and that every annotation is a
valid polygon. Comparing image hashes identified four exact duplicates, which
were removed from the annotation file; the source JPEGs were left untouched. The
working dataset is therefore 56 images with 1633 annotations over 19 categories.

The plans come from 12 distinct building projects, and several plans of the same
project are near-identical floors of the same building. A random image-level
split would place near-duplicates of one floor on both sides of the split. The
split was instead generated by a seeded search over project-level assignments,
constrained so that no project contributes images to more than one partition,
and scored on how well it preserved rare-class coverage in the test partition.
The resulting frozen split assigns 35 images to training, 10 to validation and
11 to test. It deviates from a 70/15/15 target in favour of valid rare-class
test coverage, which was judged the more useful property.

\subsubsection*{Generation strategies}

All generators read the frozen training images and nothing else. The loader
traces every augmented image back to a training identifier and rejects
validation or test provenance instead of silently filtering it out. Three
strategies were implemented.

\emph{Strategy A} gives a real rare room a different appearance of the same
class. The room polygon is rasterised, eroded slightly so that its walls are
protected, and intersected against all overlapping annotations. Dark strokes
detected inside the room are also protected, since they carry the printed label
and the room symbols. Only the remaining safe mask may be edited. A crop with
one bounding-box width of context on each side is resized to $512^2$, inpainted,
and composited back through the safe mask at original resolution. Exact
invariance outside that mask is mandatory and cannot be switched off in
configuration. Figure~\ref{fig:qastrategya} shows the audit row produced for
every candidate.

\emph{Strategy B} converts a \emph{common} room into a rare one. Common classes
are screened against the real geometry ranges of the target rare class, each
source annotation is used at most once, and the per-class cap holds the
synthetic count to three times the real count. Only the selected annotation's
category label changes. Its polygon and every other annotation stay as they are.

\emph{Strategy C} is exemplar-guided. Rather than asking the model to invent a
rare room, it transplants a real rare training room into a geometry-compatible
common-room polygon. The target interior is cleared, the donor mask is affinely
mapped onto the target, and only donor pixels are inserted. Diffusion is then
restricted to a narrow inner seam and to editable pixels the warped donor does
not cover, at low strength, so the donor pixels themselves lie outside the
diffusion mask. Three modes were generated from the same pairing plan so that
the diffusion contribution is isolated: C0 direct copy-paste, which adds no
generated pixels (arm e8); C1 deterministic feather blending (arm e9); and C2
exemplar-seeded diffusion inpainting (arms e10 and e11).
Figure~\ref{fig:qastrategyc} shows a C0 audit row.

\emph{The LoRA branch} trains one shared rank-8 adapter on the attention
projections of the inpainting U-Net, freezing the VAE, the text encoder and the
base U-Net. Training crops are room-centred crops from training annotations
only, with twelve deterministic photometric and geometric variants per usable
rare crop. Those variants are recorded as repeated appearances of the same
geometry and are never counted as new rooms. The prompt template is
``architectural technical floor plan, \{category\_name\}, 2D line drawing''. The
trained adapter then drives a common-to-rare conversion in the style of Strategy
B (arms e12 and e13). Figure~\ref{fig:loraval} compares pretrained Stable
Diffusion against the adapter on the same source, mask and seed.

Acceptance is automatic and auditable. A candidate is rejected if the editable
region is too small, if the output is blank, uniform or almost identical to the
source, if dark or saturated fill increases sharply, if the safety checker
fires, or if any pixel changed outside the editable interior.
Table~\ref{tab:acceptance} reports the outcome. These are raster checks only.
They certify that the surrounding drawing is untouched and that the edited
region is not degenerate, yet they say nothing about whether the generated
interior depicts the requested room type. Every prompt-based strategy therefore
records its semantic correctness as unverified. Strategy C is the exception,
because its rare pixels are copied from a real annotated room of that class.

\subsection{Experimental Setup and Evaluation}

Every experiment reports COCO bbox and mask AP against the untouched test
annotations \cite{lin2014coco}. The multiclass track adds a rare-macro mask AP,
the unweighted mean over categories 11--16 that have test support. The
class-agnostic track reports overall \texttt{room} AP, where rare-macro AP is
undefined. Augmented data is used for training only. Validation and test always
use the original held-out images and annotations.

\subsubsection{Goal 1: the controlled experiment matrix}

Fourteen arms were defined, listed in Table~\ref{tab:arms}. Each declares both
tracks and trains from independent ImageNet initialisation on the same recipe:
25 epochs of exactly 35 optimizer steps, SGD at learning rate 0.005 with
momentum 0.9 and weight decay $5\times10^{-4}$, the rate dropping tenfold every
8 epochs, batch size 1, aspect-preserving resize between 1024 and 1600 pixels,
mixed precision, horizontal flip and mild photometric jitter. Fixing the step
budget is what keeps the arms comparable. An
augmented arm sees a different mixture of images from e0 while receiving the
same number of gradient updates. The best checkpoint is selected on validation
mask AP.

The rare-aware sampler used by the ``$+$w'' arms assigns each training image the
bounded weight
\[
  w_i \;=\; \min\!\left(5,\; 1 + 2\sum_c \frac{n_{ic}}{\sqrt{n^{\mathrm{real}}_c}}\right),
\]
where $n_{ic}$ counts instances of class $c$ in image $i$ and
$n^{\mathrm{real}}_c$ is the class count in the original training annotations.
Using real counts stops synthetic images from inflating their own weight, and
the cap bounds any single image's sampling probability.

\subsubsection{Goal 2: the three-seed grid and the rotated held-out cross-validation}

The grid runs all 14 arms over 2 tracks and 3 seeds $\{42, 123, 2026\}$, for 84
training runs. Per-arm results are the mean and population standard deviation
over the seeds. Each augmented arm is compared to e0 by a paired test, using
Wilcoxon signed-rank where applicable and falling back to an exact two-sided
sign test. At $n=3$ the smallest attainable sign-test $p$-value is $0.25$, which
occurs only when all three seed-level differences share a sign. That ceiling is
a property of the design and is stated alongside every $p$-value in
Section~\ref{sec:results}.

The single-seed pass that preceded the grid produced apparently large effects.
Strategy A reached $0.121$ multiclass mask AP against $0.075$ for e0 on seed 42
alone, and neither number survived the addition of two further seeds. That
experience is why no single-seed number is treated as evidence anywhere in this
report.

The grid's weakness is the 11-image test split. The cross-validation addresses
it without retraining anything. The 35-image training split is held fixed for
every fold, so the augmented datasets and all grid checkpoints stay valid, and
only the evaluation set rotates. The 21 held-out images are partitioned
round-robin into $K=5$ folds, and each frozen checkpoint is re-evaluated on each
fold. Across the folds every held-out image is tested exactly once, and the
pooled rare test support rises from 17 to 40 instances. Six arms spanning the
families were selected, giving 60 evaluations. The project-aware constraint
survives this. The 21 held-out images span only three projects, so a
project-aware partition with $K>3$ is impossible, but no fold trains anything,
so the leakage that would matter is prevented by construction. Per-fold paired
tests use the same protocol, where the minimum attainable $p$-value at $K=5$ is
$0.0625$.

\subsubsection{Goal 3: SAM 3}

SAM 3 \cite{carion2025sam3} was fine-tuned with Meta's official trainer on the
unchanged split, using the 19 German category names as text concepts for the
multiclass track and a single \texttt{room} concept for the class-agnostic
track. The configuration is 20 epochs at resolution 1008 with AdamW, gradient
clipping, layer-wise learning-rate decay on the vision trunk, and separate
learning rates for the heads, the vision backbone and the language backbone. The
presence head is enabled at evaluation. Predictions are scored through the same
\texttt{pycocotools} path as the Mask R-CNN runs, so the two backbones can be
compared directly.

Three augmentation datasets were carried across without regeneration: the e10
diffusion inpaint data, the e12 LoRA data and the e8 copy-paste data. Each was
run once per track, giving a baseline plus three augmented runs in each track.
These are single-seed runs. The seed-to-seed standard deviation measured on the
grid is the noise floor against which their deltas have to be read, and
Section~\ref{sec:results} reads them that way.

\subsubsection{Goal 4: the two-stage DINOv2 pipeline}

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} draws the pipeline. Stage 1 is the already-trained
class-agnostic SAM 3 detector, which contributes 483 test proposals. Each
bounding box is expanded by a context margin, padded to a white square and
resized to $224^2$. Stage 2 encodes the crop with a frozen DINOv2-base
\cite{oquab2024dinov2} and represents it by the CLS token concatenated with the
mean of the patch tokens. Two heads were trained on those features, a linear
classifier and a cosine $k$-NN. No gradient reaches the backbone in either case.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/fig_twostage_pipeline.pdf}
\caption{The two-stage room-typing pipeline.}
\label{fig:pipeline}

Two arms differ only in what the head was trained on. One uses the 887 real
training crops. The other adds the 171 rare crops produced by the augmentation
pipeline. Two evaluations are reported. Evaluation A classifies the 368 real
ground-truth test crops, which removes detector error entirely and measures the
classification bottleneck on its own; it reports top-1 accuracy together with
macro recall over the head classes and over the rare classes. Evaluation B
labels the 483 class-agnostic proposals with the head and scores the result as
multiclass instance segmentation, which puts the two-stage system on the same
axis as the end-to-end fine-tuned detectors.

\subsection{Ethical considerations}

The dataset contains architectural drawings of real, identifiable buildings.
These are fire-brigade plans, so they mark escape routes, hydrants and shafts.
They contain no personal data and no images of people, so the exposure is
institutional rather than individual, but the plans are not public documents and
were not redistributed. All diffusion inference ran locally against a
pre-downloaded model cache with offline mode enabled, so no floor-plan image was
ever transmitted to a third-party service. The Stable Diffusion safety checker
was left enabled. It flagged and rejected a small number of candidates, which
were discarded like any other rejection.

The clearest ethical risk lies in the synthetic data. The pipeline produces
images that look like genuine fire-brigade plans but in which a room has been
silently relabelled, for example a Technikraum redrawn and annotated as a
Schacht. Such an image is safe as a training sample and unsafe as a document. If
it escaped the training directory it could be mistaken for a real plan of a real
building, with obvious consequences for anyone relying on it in an emergency.
The pipeline mitigates this by construction rather than by convention. Synthetic
images live in separate directories, carry a strategy field and a stable
synthetic identifier in their records, and are never written back into the
source dataset, which stays byte-identical throughout. This report reproduces
synthetic panels only inside labelled quality-control figures.

A second concern is honest reporting. Generation metadata records semantic
correctness as unverified wherever the automatic checks cannot establish that a
generated region depicts the requested class, and the Strategy B review file is
explicitly an automatic decision audit rather than a human review gate. Related
to this, the LoRA branch documentation records that the prompt-only conversion
retained visible common-room text labels such as ``B\"uro'' in some accepted
samples. The e12 and e13 results are reported here as measurements of the data
as it was actually used, with that caveat attached.
The project's central claim is negative, and it is reported as such, with $n$,
standard deviation and $p$-value, instead of being narrowed to whichever arms
and metrics happen to look favourable.

\subsection{Reproducibility and code availability}

All code, configuration, tests and documentation are in a single Git repository
at commit \texttt{d1b08f0}. Datasets, model weights and outputs are
machine-local and deliberately untracked. The cluster checkout lives in one
directory, \protect\path{/netscratch/espandar/inpainting/floorplan-inpainting/}, with
code in \texttt{src/}, \texttt{scripts/}, \texttt{slurm/} and
\texttt{configs/}, derived datasets in \texttt{data/}, adapters in
\texttt{models/} and every result, checkpoint and log below \texttt{outputs/}.
The consolidated summaries cited in this report sit in
\protect\path{outputs/final/}, one directory per experiment, each holding a
\texttt{summary.json} and a \texttt{summary.csv}.

Two conda environments are needed because the two backbones have incompatible
requirements: PyTorch 2.1.2 for data preparation, the diffusion generators, Mask
R-CNN and the DINOv2 stage, and PyTorch 2.10 for SAM 3. Experiments run on the
DFKI Pegasus Slurm cluster on A100 and H100 nodes, with SAM 3 jobs pinned to the
80\,GB GPU tier. Four submit scripts reproduce the whole set of results. Each is
a dependency chain that builds its inputs, smoke-tests one task, runs the full
array and then analyses the output.

At the time of writing the repository has not been pushed to a public forge, so
the cluster checkout is synchronised in place from the local \texttt{main}
branch by \protect\path{scripts/deploy_cluster.sh}, which ships the history as a Git
bundle. Once a hosted remote is configured this is replaced by an ordinary
\texttt{git pull} and the bundle mechanism can be deleted. Code availability to
a third party is therefore currently by request rather than by URL.

\subsection{Problems faced during the project}
\label{sec:problems}

\emph{The test split is too small to measure the thing the project is about.}
This is the fundamental problem, and it was not solved. Six rare categories are
scored on 17 test instances, three of them on two instances or fewer. A COCO AP
over two instances takes only a handful of discrete values, so a genuine small
improvement in rare-class segmentation is not representable, let alone
distinguishable from noise. Two mitigations were built. Three seeds measure the
noise, and the cross-validation raises the pooled rare support from 17 to 40.
Both helped characterise the measurement without removing the ceiling. The
honest statement of the outcome is that the experiment can exclude a large
rare-class effect and cannot resolve a small one.

\emph{SAM 3 training bugs.} Two defects had to be found and fixed before any SAM
3 number existed. The first was silent. Training ran, the loss printed as a
constant zero, and the backward pass raised ``does not require grad''. The
trainer resolves a loss module by a key on the collate function, and any key it
cannot match falls back to a dummy loss that returns a constant with no
gradient. The training collate function has to use the key that resolves to the
real loss wrapper, as Meta's own training configurations do. The second defect
appeared only at the first validation epoch, after the training epochs had
already been paid for. With segmentation enabled, the validation dataset yields
RLE-encoded masks, and the resize transform indexes them as tensors. The fix is
to decode the masks before any geometric transform. Both fixes are recorded as
comments in the SAM 3 configuration so they are not silently reverted.

\emph{Out-of-memory during the grid.} The first grid submission requested 32\,GB
of host memory per task, and tasks were killed during data loading rather than
on the GPU. At full drawing resolution with several workers, the decoded plans
plus the augmented index exceed that budget. The request was raised to 64\,GB,
which is sufficient.

\emph{A resume bug that corrupted optimizer state.} Runs are restartable, and
the resume path initially rebuilt the model without pretrained weights on the
assumption that the checkpoint would supply them. It does supply the weights,
but constructing the model differently changes which parameters are trainable,
so the parameter groups no longer matched the saved optimizer state. Loading
that state then failed, or worse matched positionally and restored momentum
buffers onto the wrong tensors. Model construction was made identical on both
paths, and a configuration fingerprint is now checked before a resume is
allowed.

\emph{Queue latency on the 80\,GB tier.} SAM 3 at resolution 1008 requires an
80\,GB GPU, and those partitions are the most contended available, so wall-clock
turnaround for a six-task array was dominated by queueing rather than by
compute. The mitigation was structural. Every submit script begins with one
cheap smoke task and makes the full array depend on its success, so a
configuration error costs one queued task instead of a full array of
allocations. This is also why the SAM 3 comparison is single-seed. A three-seed
SAM 3 grid was not affordable in queue time, and that limitation is carried
explicitly into the interpretation of its results.

\emph{Invalidated LoRA generation data.} Inspection of the prompt-only LoRA
conversion showed that some accepted samples kept the original common-room text
label, for example ``B\"uro'' still printed inside a room now annotated as a
rare class. The mask construction protects detected dark strokes, and those
strokes include exactly the labels that identify the old class. A revised
generator was implemented that masks compact interior labels while preserving
structural lines and seeds generation with a real rare exemplar. The e12 and e13
arms and the SAM 3 LoRA run reported here use the original prompt-only data, so
they are best read as evidence about that generator rather than about
class-conditioned LoRA inpainting in general.
